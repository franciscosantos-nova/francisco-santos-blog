Almost two thousand years ago, the most powerful man in the world sat in a
military camp on the edge of the Roman Empire and wrote notes to himself.
Not speeches, not laws, not letters. Private reminders, in Greek, about
how to think and how to live.

Marcus Aurelius never meant anyone to read them. The title we now give
them, *Meditations*, came later. The original was closer to "To Himself".
It was a personal record, kept in the middle of war, plague and political
chaos, of the principles he was trying to live by.

I went back to the *Meditations* this month, after a plan I had been
working towards for a long time fell apart (Exchange in Hong Kong). I
picked the book up looking for perspective, not for blockchain ideas. But
the more I read, the more I kept seeing the same questions I have been
writing about here: what can we control, what can we trust, and what
happens when the real world does not follow the rules we wrote.

This blog is, in a way, the opposite of his notebook, a public record of
what I'm learning. So it felt right to bring the two together. Here are
four ideas from the "Real Stoic" that explain more about blockchains, and
about Real World Assets, than most whitepapers.

## 1. Know what is in your power

The idea Marcus Aurelius returns to more than any other is simple. Some
things are in our power, and most things are not. Our judgments, our
choices and our actions belong to us. Other people, illness, war and luck
do not. In Book 8 he puts it this way:

> If thou art pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that
> disturbs thee, but thy own judgement about it. And it is in thy power
> to wipe out this judgement now.

For the Stoics, most suffering comes from confusing the two. Spending
energy on what we can't change, and neglecting what we can.

A blockchain lives by the same concepts. Inside its walls, it is
sovereign. Who owns which token, under which rules, and every transfer
ever made is decided by code, and nothing outside can disturb it. But the
moment a token points to something in the real world, the blockchain goes
outside "its power". It can not guard a vault, enforce a contract or stop
a company from going bankrupt.

In my [last post](/posts/the-anatomy-of-a-real-world-asset), I took a
tokenized Pokémon card apart layer by layer. Only one of those six
layers, the token itself, actually lives on the blockchain. The other
five all live outside it.

Good projects do what a Stoic would do, they master what is on-chain, and
they prepare with humility for everything that is not, through
contracts, insurance, audits and independent verification.

## 2. The past can not be edited

The Stoics were relentless about time. In Book 3, Marcus reminds himself
that:

> Every man lives only this present time, which is an indivisible point,
> and that all the rest of his life is either past or it is uncertain.

The past is fixed. Regretting it, replaying it or wishing it had gone
differently changes nothing. The only thing we ever control is the next
action, taken now.

A blockchain is built on the same principle. Every block is linked to the
one before it, so changing anything in the past would mean rewriting
everything that came after, in front of thousands of computers that all
keep a copy. In practice, history is final. That is not a limitation, it
is the whole point. You can trust the record precisely because nobody
can quietly change it.

It also means mistakes are not erased, they are corrected. If a token is
sent to the wrong wallet, you can not undo the transaction. You can only
add a new one that fixes it, and both remain visible forever.

For Real World Assets, this is where much of the value lies. Anyone can
open a block explorer like Etherscan and look at BlackRock's BUIDL fund:
Every token created, every transfer between investors… No annual report
or press release needed. Even when an issuer freezes a token or takes it
back, the blockchain records that too. The past can not be hidden, so the
next decision has to be a good one.

## 3. Examine every impression before you accept it

The Stoics believed that the world constantly throws "impressions" at
us, a rumour, an insult, a sudden fear. The mistake is not having them,
it is accepting them without question. Before giving your assent, you
are supposed to stop and look at the thing as it really is. Marcus
Aurelius reminds himself to:

> Make for thyself a definition or description of the thing which is
> presented to thee, so as to see distinctly what kind of a thing it is
> in its substance, in its nudity, in its complete entirety

In other words, do not react to the story. Examine the facts first.

A smart contract has the opposite behaviour. It never questions
anything. Whatever data it receives, it acts on immediately and
perfectly. If the price it is given is wrong, it will execute perfectly
on a lie. This is why the information coming from outside the
blockchain, through what are called oracles, is one of its weakest
points.

For Real World Assets, this problem is everywhere, because almost
everything that matters about the asset lives off chain. The question is
always the same: What is the asset worth today? A well designed RWA does
what a Stoic does with an impression, it does not accept a single source
at face value. It checks prices across several independent feeds, relies
on audits, and builds in pauses when something looks wrong.

The blockchain can not think, so the people designing it have to do the
thinking for it, before the impression becomes an action.

## 4. Rules are not a substitute for character

Marcus Aurelius had more power than almost anyone in history. There was
no court above him, no election to lose, no rule he could not change. And
yet his notebook is full of reminders to hold himself back: To be fair,
to be modest, to avoid becoming the kind of tyrant Rome had seen before.
Nobody could force him to be good. He had to choose it, every day. In
Book 10, he tells himself:

> No longer talk at all about the kind of man that a good man ought to
> be, but be such.

Blockchains were born from the opposite idea. Instead of hoping people
behave well, you write the rules into code, so that nobody has to be
trusted. The system does not care about anyone's character, it just
executes.

But as the previous ideas showed, Real World Assets never fully escape
the human side. Someone holds the card in the vault, someone values the
asset, someone decides whether a court enforces the contract. And often,
someone holds special powers over the token itself, the so called admin
keys, which allow them to freeze, upgrade or take back tokens. Like an
Emperor, the holder of those keys can do a lot, and the only thing
stopping them from abusing it is a mix of rules, reputation and
character.

So, code does not remove the need for trust. It reduces how much of it we
need, and makes it visible where it remains. The best RWA projects do not
pretend to be "trustless". They are honest about who holds the power, put
limits around it, and make sure someone is watching.

Even the most powerful system still depends, somewhere, on someone
choosing to be good.

## What remains in our power

If we put the four ideas together, we get something close to a Stoic
design manual for Real World Assets. Know what the code controls and
what it doesn't. Trust the record because it can't be rewritten. Never
accept outside data without checking it. And remember that behind every
token, somewhere, there is still a person who has to choose to do the
right thing.

None of this is new. Marcus Aurelius wrote it nearly two thousand years
ago, in a tent, for no one but himself. The technology is different, the
problem isn't. Every system of trust, from an empire to a blockchain,
eventually runs into the same limit: the world does not follow the rules
we write.

I started this month with a plan that fell apart, my biggest dream, my
main goal, going to Hong Kong for an Exchange semester, but sadly I
couldn't change that. That part of the record is final. What I can
control is the next play. This blog is one of them.
